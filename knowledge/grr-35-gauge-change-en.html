Why does a GRR problem persist after replacing the gauge?

When a GRR result fails, start improvement from the source of variation. Variance components, sample selection, NDC, and destructive-testing scenarios turn a GRR number into the correct investigation path.

About 5 min readMeasurement System AnalysisUpdated 2026-08-07
GRRMSAMeasurement System AnalysisGaugeRepeatabilityReproducibilityNDC

Quick takeaways

  • When GRR fails, start with the variance components to see whether variation comes mainly from repeatability (EV), reproducibility (AV), or part variation (PV).
  • When AV dominates, check operator methods and work instructions first instead of replacing the gauge.
  • Sample selection, NDC, and destructive-testing design also determine whether a GRR conclusion is valid.

Core conclusion

A GRR result describes the variation the measurement system currently shows. Improvement should start from the variance components and problem location, not from replacing the gauge with a higher-precision instrument.

Engineering note

Replacing the gauge cannot fix variation caused by operator methods, sample representativeness, or test conditions.

Common misconception

Completing “10 parts × 3 operators × 2 trials” does not by itself make an MSA valid. Sample range, data structure, and test characteristics determine whether the study is meaningful.

When a customer is waiting for the MSA report and the GRR result comes back at 35%, many quality engineers immediately move into improvement mode. Confirming gauge calibration, checking instrument precision, and requesting a higher-grade measurement device are usually the first actions considered.

This approach is common on the shop floor, because the measuring device is the most visible part of a measurement system. In real projects, however, the reason behind a failing GRR is often hidden in other parts of the measurement process. Without first understanding the source of variation, improvement actions can easily stay on the surface.

A 35% GRR result can hide completely different problems

In one similar case, an inspection laboratory measured precision shaft diameters with micrometers. After the GRR study, the result reached 35%, exceeding the customer requirement. The project team scheduled a gauge calibration the same day, hoping to confirm whether degraded instrument precision had increased measurement error. The calibration showed the gauge was normal, but a repeat GRR still stayed around 35%.

The problem returned to its starting point.

The engineers then reviewed the ANOVA and variance-component results again and found that repeatability (EV) was not the main contributor. The largest source of variation was reproducibility (AV). A closer look at the three operators’ measurement processes showed that their preparation before measuring differed.

One operator let the part sit in the environment until its temperature approached a stable state before measuring. The other two operators measured the part immediately after receiving it. For ordinary dimension measurement, this difference may be hard to notice. For precision shaft diameters, however, the small dimensional change caused by temperature is large enough to affect the result.

The real problem was an incomplete measurement-method definition. The work instruction did not specify a temperature-stabilization requirement before measurement, so each operator developed a different habit based on experience. Those differences eventually appeared in the GRR result.

This case shows that a GRR report describes the variation level the measurement system currently exhibits. Follow-up improvement requires analyzing where that variation comes from. Behind a 35% GRR result, the engineering problems can be completely different.

Move from the GRR number back to the sources of variation

In MSA, GRR is the combined expression of repeatability and reproducibility. Repeatability (EV) reflects the variation when one operator repeats measurements with the same device under the same conditions. Reproducibility (AV) reflects differences between operators. Part-to-part variation (PV) represents the real differences among the samples themselves.

So when a GRR result is abnormal, the first step is to review the variance components to identify the dominant contributor, and only then decide the direction of investigation.

When EV dominates

Investigation usually focuses on the measurement device and method, such as gauge stability, resolution, fixture positioning, measurement environment, or variation in operating steps.

When AV dominates

The focus shifts to people and process, such as whether operators use different measurement postures, interpret the measurement requirement differently, or work from a work instruction with ambiguous areas.

When operator-by-part interaction is present

Confirm whether certain parts have special characteristics, such as surface condition, feature location, or dimension distribution, that cause different people to obtain different results.

In Blendex MSA, GRR analysis does not stop at the final %GRR value. It also shows the ANOVA, variance components, and the contribution ratios of EV, AV, interaction, and part variation, so engineers can move from the result into problem location instead of receiving only a pass or fail conclusion.

Some failing GRR results come from sample selection

Beyond equipment, people, and method, sample selection is an often-overlooked part of MSA.

A GRR study relies on differences between samples to judge whether the measurement system can identify real variation. If the selected parts are very similar—for example, all ten samples come from one stable batch with dimensions concentrated in a very small range—part variation will be limited.

In that situation, the variation produced by the measurement system itself is relatively amplified, so the GRR ratio rises.

This happens frequently on site: the engineer completes 10 parts, 3 operators, and 2 trials as required, but the result is still not acceptable. A closer check shows that the selected samples did not cover the variation range of the real production process.

An effective MSA study must reflect the real manufacturing state of the product. Samples should represent normal process variation, including states near the specification limits and differences that can appear across batches and production conditions.

If the samples do not reflect process variation, the conclusions of the measurement-system study will also be limited.

%GRR passes, but can the measurement system really support engineering judgment?

In some projects, engineers focus on whether %GRR meets the requirement, while paying less attention to NDC (Number of Distinct Categories).

For example, a measurement system with a %GRR of only 8% looks excellent from a ratio perspective, but its NDC is only 3.

This means the system repeats consistently, but its ability to distinguish real differences between parts is limited.

For a simple release decision, such a measurement system may still be usable. But if the company wants to use the data for process improvement, supplier capability assessment, or SPC management, the measurement system must be re-evaluated to confirm it can provide enough information.

The value of a measurement system is not only stable repeat measurement; it is also the ability to help the engineering team identify real product changes.

MSA for destructive testing requires a redesigned study

There is one special situation on the shop floor: destructive testing.

Dimension measurement can repeat the same part several times, but for tensile tests, peel tests, or drop tests, the sample is destroyed after the first test, so the traditional repeated-measurement GRR method cannot be used.

In this case, the MSA plan must be redesigned around the test characteristics.

Common practice uses paired samples: select a group of samples manufactured under as similar conditions as possible for comparison, or use specialized analysis methods designed for destructive testing. The study design must fit the actual test conditions while preserving the ability to judge the sources of measurement-system variation.

Engineering judgment behind a GRR report

On the shop floor, MSA is often treated as a required item for project introduction, customer audits, or quality documentation. For engineering teams truly responsible for product quality, the meaning of MSA is to confirm whether measurement data can truthfully reflect product state.

When GRR is abnormal, follow-up improvement must be based on an understanding of the variation sources. Equipment condition, operator practice, measurement method, sample selection, and test conditions can all influence the result.

Going back to the shaft-measurement case: if the team had replaced the gauge immediately after seeing the 35% GRR result, the problem would probably still exist. The real cause was a measurement condition that was not written into the work instruction: whether the part must reach a stable temperature before measurement.

The final improvement was not a more precise micrometer. It was redefining the measurement process so that all operators follow the same method.

This is the most important role of MSA on the shop floor. It helps teams find the source behind abnormal measurement data and makes the measurement process itself more stable and repeatable.
