When AI Enters the Factory Floor: How Can General-Purpose LLMs Gain Engineering Judgment?

Based on a 2026 study on domain adaptation of large language models for additive manufacturing, this article explains why general-purpose LLMs struggle to support engineering judgment directly, and how RAG and fine-tuning help models acquire engineering capability.

About 11 min readAI & Quality EngineeringUpdated 2026-08-07
Large Language ModelRAGFine-tuningEngineering JudgmentDomain AdaptationManufacturing AIAI Quality

Quick takeaways

  • Engineering problems are far more complex than information organisation; general-purpose LLMs without domain knowledge constraints cannot directly support manufacturing decisions.
  • Engineering knowledge lives not only in documents, but also in how engineers interpret information.
  • The study shows that fine-tuning directly on raw domain text brought limited gains, while RAG performed better in accuracy, relevance and expert preference.
  • RAG is well suited as a foundation capability for engineering AI: knowledge sources are traceable and updatable without retraining the whole model.
  • RAG and fine-tuning are not either/or; the longer-term direction is a hybrid architecture combining both.
  • Critical quality decisions still depend on the engineering accountability system; AI is better used as an assistant for engineering knowledge.

Core conclusion

The core of engineering AI is not making the model more articulate, but giving it access to trusted, controlled and updatable knowledge sources and helping it understand the context behind engineering problems.

Engineering note

A statistical indicator (such as Cpk = 1.25) supports engineering judgment only when combined with the measurement system, data source, process stability and risk level.

Common misconception

Having more documents does not mean AI-usable engineering knowledge. Knowledge must be structured and keep its basis of judgment to be retrieved and reused.

In quality and manufacturing settings, using AI to automatically summarise an 8D report, extract key information from inspection reports, or help engineers quickly condense meeting notes has become an increasingly common application. However, when AI begins to take part in more complex engineering tasks — such as judging whether a supplier’s dimensional anomaly may escalate into a batch risk, analysing whether an improvement action truly eliminated a systemic cause, or evaluating whether a process change may introduce new failure modes — companies quickly discover that engineering problems are far more complex than information organisation.

Quality engineers rarely rely on a single data point to make a judgment. A supplier nonconformance analysis usually needs to combine product risk, key characteristics, process capability, measurement system status, historical performance and improvement verification results. A manufacturing process adjustment also needs to consider materials, equipment, process windows and on-site constraints. Engineering judgment depends on the relationships among large amounts of scattered information, as well as experience accumulated over time.

This is also the problem manufacturers gradually encounter after adopting large language models (LLMs). General-purpose models have strong language understanding and generation capabilities, but in professional engineering domains, general knowledge alone cannot directly support complex manufacturing decisions. What companies need to solve is how to help AI understand the background, constraints and reasoning logic behind engineering problems.

The 2026 research paper published in Frontiers in Manufacturing Technology, “Domain adaptation of large language models for additive manufacturing using retrieval-augmented generation and fine-tuning”, studies exactly this problem. Using additive manufacturing (AM) as the research scenario, the authors compared three approaches — a base large language model, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and fine-tuning — in professional engineering question answering. The study focuses on how to adapt general-purpose language models to highly specialised engineering knowledge domains.

Why general-purpose LLMs still fall short in engineering domains

General-purpose LLMs, trained on large-scale data, show strong capabilities in text understanding, knowledge summarisation and language generation. However, knowledge in manufacturing engineering has distinctive characteristics: it is usually scattered across technical standards, equipment documentation, process specifications, experimental data, research papers and internal company experience, and there are many dependencies among these sources.

The paper notes that knowledge sources in additive manufacturing include academic literature, manufacturer materials, technical standards and operating guides. Although general LLMs have strong language ability, the lack of professional domain knowledge constraints and structured technical knowledge support can lead to answers that are insufficiently accurate, lack detail, or fail to connect with actual scenarios.

The same situation exists in quality engineering.

For example, a general-purpose model can explain the definition of Cpk and describe the basic 8D process. But when a quality engineer faces a real problem — such as whether a supplier’s key dimension with a Cpk of 1.25 is acceptable — the question already goes beyond explaining an indicator.

Engineers need to further understand whether this dimension is a critical-to-quality characteristic (CTQ), how customer requirements and risk levels are defined, whether the data comes from a stable process, whether the measurement system has sufficient capability, and whether the supplier has had similar failure modes in the past.

Based on the basic principles of process capability analysis and measurement system analysis, a statistical indicator must be interpreted together with the data source, measurement system capability and the actual application context. Looking at a single Cpk value cannot fully reflect process risk.

Therefore, the engineering AI manufacturers really need is not simply a model that can answer technical questions, but an assistant system that can understand problems in the context of specific engineering situations.

How engineering knowledge moves from documents to usable capability

Manufacturers usually have accumulated large amounts of technical material, including quality manuals, SOPs, 8D reports, PFMEAs, control plans, customer specifications and historical nonconformance records. These materials contain a great deal of experience and knowledge, but an increasing number of documents does not mean the enterprise already has engineering knowledge that AI can use directly.

The reason is that engineering knowledge exists not only in document content, but also in how engineers understand this information.

For example, a supplier 8D report may record the defect phenomenon, root cause analysis and improvement actions. But an experienced SQE usually asks deeper questions: does this root cause cover the true systemic cause? Do the improvement actions target the mechanism? Is the verification approach strong enough to prove the risk has been reduced? Could similar problems occur again in the future?

These judgments are often not fully recorded in any single document; they are scattered across engineering experience, historical cases and team collaboration.

The paper validates this problem. The researchers used LLaMA-3-8B as the base model and built three models for comparison: the base model, a RAG model and a fine-tuned model. For fine-tuning, QLoRA was used to adjust model parameters so the model could learn additive manufacturing domain text knowledge.

The experimental results showed that, under this study setting, directly fine-tuning parameters on raw domain text did not bring obvious performance gains. In accuracy comparisons, only 5.6% of the fine-tuned model’s answers were rated by experts as better than the base model, and in relevance the proportion was 32.5%.

The paper further points out that this result is related to the training approach. Directly feeding large amounts of raw domain text mainly exposes the model to domain facts and technical descriptions, but does not provide demonstrations of how to handle engineering problems, how to organise reasoning, or how to form task-relevant answers. Under these study conditions, adding domain text input did not automatically translate into engineering question-answering capability.

It is important to note that this does not mean fine-tuning has no value in engineering AI. For scenarios that already have high-quality engineering Q&A data, clear task formats and stable decision processes, instruction fine-tuning can still help the model produce outputs that better match enterprise needs. The paper validates the effect of fine-tuning on raw domain text; it does not reject all domain adaptation methods.

Why RAG is suitable as a foundation capability for engineering AI

The paper focuses on the RAG approach. Unlike directly adjusting model parameters, RAG retrieves relevant content from an external knowledge base during answering and uses it as reference when the model generates a response.

The paper notes that RAG can use retrieved domain materials to improve factual accuracy, reduce the risk of hallucinated or erroneous output, and continuously absorb new knowledge by updating the knowledge base, without retraining the entire model.

In the study, the authors built an additive manufacturing domain knowledge base containing more than 250 peer-reviewed papers, over 30 book chapters, more than 150 process guides, over 50 international technical standards and extensive material data resources.

At the same time, the research team designed 200 expert-level questions to evaluate the models on material selection, process parameter optimisation, defect diagnosis and understanding of manufacturing processes.

The results showed that the RAG model outperformed the base model on several evaluation metrics. For overall answer preference, experts preferred RAG-generated answers in 85.2% of cases; for technical accuracy, 75.5% of RAG answers were considered more accurate; and for relevance, RAG reached 90.8%.

For manufacturers, this result indicates that one of the key capabilities of engineering AI is enabling the model to access trusted, controlled and continuously updated knowledge sources.

Manufacturing knowledge is constantly changing. Standards are updated, processes are adjusted, supplier status changes and enterprise experience keeps accumulating. Therefore, retrieving from a controlled knowledge base while preserving knowledge sources and evidence better matches the reliability requirements of engineering environments.

In practice, RAG should not be understood as a replacement for fine-tuning. For manufacturers, the longer-term direction may be to combine both capabilities: use RAG to ensure knowledge sources and traceability, and use task-specific instruction optimisation to improve output format and engineering expression, forming a hybrid architecture better suited to professional scenarios.

From additive manufacturing to quality engineering: engineering AI faces the same kind of problem

Although this paper studies the additive manufacturing domain, its conclusions are relevant to quality engineering.

Process optimisation, material selection and defect analysis in additive manufacturing share a common feature with Cpk judgment, MSA evaluation, 8D root cause analysis and supplier risk management in quality engineering: engineering conclusions usually cannot be derived from a single piece of data, but require placing the data into a specific production context.

For example, in additive manufacturing, a defect may relate jointly to material state, temperature parameters, interlayer bonding and equipment conditions. In quality engineering, a process capability indicator also needs to be interpreted together with measurement system capability, product risk level, control plan and historical performance.

Therefore, both kinds of problems depend on context.

Quality engineering has long been rich in data, including measurement results, inspection reports, SPC data, MSA analyses, 8D documents and supplier quality records. However, this information usually exists as separate files, lacking connections to product background, process conditions and engineering judgment.

For example, a process capability report may show a Cpk of 1.25 for a dimension. This result reflects the statistical performance of the process relative to specification limits under the current data conditions, but the QE still needs to judge whether the measurement system is reliable, whether the data comes from a stable process, whether the characteristic is a high-risk CTQ, and whether the supplier’s control plan can effectively prevent risk escalation.

Similarly, a supplier nonconformance should not be evaluated only by its closure status. Information with real long-term value also includes the problem background, failure mode, analysis path, improvement actions and verification results.

If this information can be managed in a structured way, AI can help engineers quickly reuse historical experience and support more consistent engineering judgment.

The application boundary of AI in quality engineering

The study shows that the RAG model performed better in contextual correctness, comprehensibility and practical value, because it can generate answers based on external knowledge sources.

For QEs and SQEs, AI can handle a large amount of information work, such as retrieving historical cases, linking relevant standards, organising nonconformance patterns, analysing quality trends and assisting in generating preliminary analysis results.

However, critical decisions in manufacturing quality still need to rest on the engineering accountability system.

Whether a product is released, whether a significant quality risk is accepted, and whether a supplier’s improvement actions are effective — these judgments must be completed with reliable data, verification results and engineering experience.

Therefore, AI in the quality domain is better suited as an assistant for engineering knowledge, helping engineers reduce time spent searching for information and repeating analyses, so more effort can go into problems that require professional judgment.

When companies build quality AI in the future, what matters is not only model capability, but also knowledge sources, data structures, rule systems and the traceability of the judgment process.

Making engineering experience a capability the enterprise can reuse

This paper studies how to adapt large language models to the additive manufacturing domain, but its value is not limited to one manufacturing technology. Manufacturers have accumulated a great deal of engineering experience over time, and this experience often depends on individuals, teams or even a few key engineers. When people leave, the enterprise may also lose a large amount of tacit knowledge.

One direction of quality digitalisation is to gradually turn this experience into engineering knowledge the organisation can preserve, retrieve and reuse.

The previous discussion on Smart Metrology focused on how to make measurement data trustworthy quality evidence. This study extends to another question: once an enterprise has more and more quality evidence, how can AI understand this evidence and help engineers make more reliable judgments?

For manufacturers, the application of AI ultimately depends on the enterprise’s ability to build a foundation that connects data, domain knowledge and engineering judgment. When engineering data, quality evidence and professional experience can be managed in a structured way, AI truly has the foundation to support work on the factory floor.
