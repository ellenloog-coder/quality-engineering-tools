When Quality Data Becomes a Decision Asset: From Smart Metrology to the Quality Evidence Chain

Drawing on a 2026 Smart Metrology review, this article explains how measurement enters the manufacturing decision chain, the four levels of quality information interoperability, and the value of moving from report traceability to decision traceability.

About 15 min readAI & Quality EngineeringUpdated 2026-08-07
Smart MetrologyDigital ThreadQuality DataInteroperabilityQuality Evidence ChainDecision TraceabilityAI Quality

Quick takeaways

  • Quality data is moving from result records to decision assets; the core shift is letting measurement results enter the manufacturing decision chain.
  • Digital quality maturity cannot be measured by device connectivity rates alone; what matters is whether engineering meaning survives across systems.
  • Semantic interoperability is a critical breaking point for the Digital Thread: a file opening is not the same as engineering definitions staying consistent.
  • AI can strengthen quality judgment, but product release, risk acceptance and accountability still require trustworthy measurement and controlled specifications.
  • A quality evidence chain answers why a judgment was made, going beyond report traceability.
  • Small and medium manufacturers can start by unifying base quality fields, retaining analysis conditions and recording engineering judgments.

Core conclusion

Quality data becomes a decision asset when measurement results, analysis conditions, engineering judgments and verification outcomes are continuously linked — not merely when what happened is recorded.

Engineering note

A statistical indicator (such as Cpk = 1.18) cannot serve as decision evidence on its own; it must be kept together with the measurement system, data source, analysis conditions and engineering context.

Common misconception

System connectivity is not quality information interoperability. A file can transfer and open, but Datum and tolerance semantics may still break downstream.

For decades, quality management in manufacturing has revolved around one core goal: confirming that products meet defined requirements. From defining dimensions, tolerances and performance indicators at the design stage, to inspection and testing during production, to final product release through quality reports, measurement systems have long played the role of verifying conformity. Companies build their quality control systems on inspection records, test reports, SPC, MSA, process capability analysis and nonconformance reports — and this mechanism still works today.

What is really changing is the value companies expect from quality data. As product complexity, manufacturing precision, supply chain coordination and compliance requirements continue to rise, companies are no longer asking only whether a product is conforming. They also want to understand why quality variation happens, where risk is forming, whether the current judgment is well supported, and whether similar problems can be identified earlier in the future. Quality data is therefore shifting from result records to decision assets.

The 2026 review paper published in Measurement: Sensors, “A review of smart metrology in industry 4.0–5.0: Standards, interoperability, and automation”, systematically examines this trend. The paper points out that dimensional metrology is moving from a traditional post-process inspection activity to an intelligent measurement system within digital manufacturing, connecting design, manufacturing and quality assurance through standardised information models, automation, AI and the Digital Thread.

Smart Metrology redefines where measurement sits in manufacturing

In traditional manufacturing, measurement is usually located at the back end of the production flow. After a part is machined, quality personnel obtain data through CMMs, gauges, optical equipment or other inspection means, and then judge conformity against specification requirements. In this model, measurement plays a verification role, and the data mainly serves release, rework or nonconformance analysis.

The change embodied by Smart Metrology is not simply adding more advanced sensors or automated inspection equipment. It is about letting measurement results enter the manufacturing decision chain. The paper describes modern measurement systems as networked, information-rich manufacturing nodes that continuously generate, exchange and interpret data, and connect with design, production and enterprise systems.

In this future quality system, an isolated “10.03 mm” should therefore not be just a measurement result. It may simultaneously correspond to a design feature, a specific revision of tolerance requirements, a manufacturing process, a measuring device, a production batch, and the engineering judgments made afterwards. Only when this information can be continuously linked does a number truly become quality information usable by engineering systems.

This information chain can be understood as:

Design intent (STEP AP242) → Manufacturing and process information → Equipment and system communication (OPC UA) → Inspection plans and results (QIF) → Engineering judgment and decision evidence

The paper places particular emphasis on the role of the Digital Thread. STEP AP242 can carry product geometry, PMI (Product and Manufacturing Information) and assembly relationships, while QIF can describe inspection plans, measurement results and related resources. Together, they connect design requirements with actual inspection evidence and maintain traceability relationships across the product lifecycle through persistent identifiers.

From a quality engineering perspective, the value of the Digital Thread is not putting all data into a single database, but minimising the reinterpretation and re-entry of engineering information between stages. When design requirements change, downstream inspection needs to know which characteristics are affected. When inspection results drift continuously, the engineering team also needs to know the corresponding design requirement and manufacturing process for that characteristic. Measurement therefore shifts from a post-production confirmation activity to an engineering information node throughout the product lifecycle.

Data can be transferred, but quality information is not necessarily interoperable

Manufacturing digitalisation often treats system connectivity as the main goal, but the paper raises a question more deserving of attention from quality engineering teams: data exchange is only the starting point of interoperability. The real difficulty is whether different systems can understand quality information in a consistent way.

The paper divides Smart Metrology interoperability into four levels:

Interoperability levelWhat it focuses on
Technical InteroperabilityConcerned with whether devices, sensors and systems can establish physical and communication connections; for example, OPC UA, MTConnect, industrial networks and device adapters mainly address this level.
Syntactic InteroperabilityConcerned with whether different systems can exchange information according to a unified data structure; for example, standards such as STEP AP242, QIF and STEP-NC help systems understand which fields exist in a file and how they should be organised.
Semantic InteroperabilityFurther concerned with whether the engineering meaning represented by these fields stays consistent. For example, when a Datum, Feature, Tolerance or Characteristic moves from design software into an inspection system, does it still represent the original engineering definition?
Organizational InteroperabilityEnters the enterprise governance level, covering data ownership, version control, approval authority, cross-factory responsibility and lifecycle change management.

This distinction explains why some companies already have large numbers of connected devices and digital platforms, yet still struggle to form truly continuous quality information. A file being transferred does not mean its engineering meaning has been fully carried over.

For example, a STEP AP242 or QIF file may open normally in two systems, which means the syntactic level is connected. But if the definition of a Datum, feature identifiers or tolerance context changes during conversion, the semantic level is already broken. The paper explicitly notes that even when different tools can exchange standardised files, if feature identifiers, datum information or tolerance semantics cannot stay consistent in downstream tools, the Digital Thread can still break.

When companies further expand to multiple plants, suppliers or contract manufacturers, the problem also enters the organisational level. If Plant A produces to Specification Rev. B while Plant B still uses Rev. A; if a supplier has updated its inspection procedure but the OEM’s approved version has not been synchronised; or if different sites have different modification authority over inspection plans — even when all systems are technically connected, quality governance can still fail.

Therefore, one important lesson Smart Metrology offers manufacturers is that digital quality maturity cannot be measured only by device connectivity rate or number of integrated systems. What is really worth evaluating is whether the engineering meaning of quality information remains complete after passing through different systems and different organisations.

From automated inspection to closed-loop control: quality participates in manufacturing decisions earlier

Another important direction of Smart Metrology is shortening the distance between measurement and process adjustment. Traditional quality flows wait for inspection to finish, then engineers analyse deviations, judge risk and adjust equipment or process. In high-throughput, high-precision manufacturing, this can mean significant feedback delay.

Closed-loop Quality Control, as discussed in the paper, attempts to connect measurement results directly to manufacturing control processes. When an inspection system detects a deviation, the information can be analysed and fed back to machining or assembly control systems to adjust feed rate, tool path, clamping force or other manufacturing parameters.

This change means quality systems are moving from simply detecting out-of-specification conditions towards identifying drift and predicting risk. A dimension still within specification does not mean the process is healthy. If continuous measurement data shows the centre gradually shifting, a future quality system may trigger process checks or engineering intervention before an actual out-of-specification event occurs.

For QEs, this means future work will increasingly focus on whether signals are trustworthy, whether the measurement system is stable enough, and what kind of change should trigger engineering action. For SQEs, supplier quality management may also move from periodically receiving inspection reports towards continuously assessing process capability, measurement trustworthiness and risk changes.

AI can enhance quality judgment, but it cannot bypass Measurement Trust

AI is another important component of Smart Metrology. The paper reviews applications such as surface defect recognition, dynamic error compensation, inspection path optimisation, anomaly screening and predictive diagnostics, while also pointing out the limitations of AI in metrology scenarios.

Quality scenarios are clearly different from general prediction scenarios. An AI model’s output may affect product release, process compensation, inspection strategy or even customer risk. Companies therefore need to know not only what the model output, but also what data the output was based on, which model version was used, under what conditions it ran, and why the current judgment was produced.

The paper points out that imbalanced training data, overfitting and a lack of Ground Truth can all lead to unreliable predictions. As AI output moves closer to actual quality actions, requirements for explainability, uncertainty expression, model version control and auditable traceability will also increase.

This effectively brings the Audit Trail into the discussion of AI Quality. Traditional quality reports need to keep records of data, measuring equipment, personnel and time. Once AI is involved, the audit objects further expand to model versions, data used for calls, rule versions and output results. Whether AI can explain its conclusions is one thing; more importantly, companies need to be able to answer in the future: “What information did this model actually see, and on what basis did it give this recommendation?”

Therefore, the application boundaries of AI for QEs and SQEs need to remain clear. AI is well suited to large-scale data screening, anomaly early warning, historical case retrieval, knowledge retrieval and assisted risk identification, because these tasks significantly reduce the time engineers spend searching for information and repeating analyses. However, for final product release, acceptance of significant quality risk, approval of supplier corrective actions and determination of engineering accountability, judgment still needs to be based on trustworthy measurement, controlled specifications, clear engineering rules and the corresponding approval mechanisms.

The real lesson of Smart Metrology for large manufacturers: quality systems must move from a collection of tools to a continuous system

For companies that already have a relatively strong digital foundation, the lesson of this paper is not to purchase another intelligent measurement system, but to re-examine data continuity among design, manufacturing, inspection and quality systems.

Large manufacturers often already have PLM, MES, ERP, laboratory systems, CMMs, AOI or other automated inspection equipment. The real challenge is usually not a lack of systems, but whether information across these systems shares a unified engineering semantics, and whether historical quality evidence can continue to be used across the product lifecycle.

From this perspective, the value of standards such as QIF, STEP AP242, OPC UA and ISA-95 is not only technical compatibility, but helping companies gradually build shared data models and a governance foundation. The paper notes that much research and industrial practice currently concentrates on technical, syntactic and semantic interoperability, while organisational governance remains relatively weak, including issues such as data ownership, model versioning, cross-site changes and release authority.

Small and medium manufacturers do not need to wait for a complete digital factory to begin

Smart Metrology describes a highly integrated future manufacturing architecture, but many companies in reality do not have complete PLM, MES, digital twins or automated measurement networks. Many small and medium manufacturers still rely mainly on Excel, inspection reports, test records, email, 8D and engineer experience for daily quality work.

This does not mean they have no traceability capability. In fact, a normal quality report usually already records product, part number, batch, specification, measuring equipment, date, inspector and test results. These are basic tasks in a quality system.

What is really worth improving is the continuity between these records. A company may be able to find out who inspected a batch, but may not be able to quickly confirm which specification revision was in use at the time; it may be able to find a Cpk report, but may not be able to reconfirm which data range and analysis conditions were used; it may be able to find a past 8D, but may not know why an improvement was accepted, or whether subsequent performance verified the original judgment.

Companies can start by unifying base quality fields across products and suppliers, so that product, characteristic, batch, specification source and data source are recorded in a consistent way; retain key analysis conditions and version information in Cpk, MSA, SPC or DOE analyses, so historical results can be re-understood; and include final engineering judgments and their basis in formal records, instead of leaving them only in email, meetings or personal memory.

There is still an important distance between quality traceability and the quality evidence chain

Traditional quality traceability mainly answers what happened and who did it, such as when a batch was produced, when it was inspected and who approved it. The quality evidence chain needs to further answer why this judgment was made and whether the evidence supporting it is trustworthy.

Suppose an SQE receives a capability analysis report during a supplier nonconformance, showing a Cpk of 1.18 for a key dimension. If only this number is kept, it cannot independently explain the full engineering risk.

From a statistical analysis perspective, engineers at least need to understand which production stage the data came from, whether the specification limits are correct, whether the process shows obvious instability signals, and whether the capability analysis method used is appropriate for the data. At the same time, if the measurement system itself has significant repeatability or reproducibility problems, an isolated Cpk = 1.18 is hard to use as complete decision evidence.

Therefore, evidence with real engineering meaning is not a single statistical indicator, but the relationship between that indicator and the measurement system, data source, analysis conditions and engineering context. If quality records can simultaneously link product stage, key characteristics, specification revision, measurement system status, analysis method, major risk judgment, actions taken and subsequent verification results, then this nonconformance is no longer just a historical file, but evidence that can be reused for future engineering decisions.

Moving from report traceability to decision traceability may be the more realistic starting point for intelligent quality

The next stage of quality digitalisation may not first appear as more equipment or more software, but as a more fundamental change: a report can not only prove that a result happened, but also explain how the result was produced.

DimensionReport TraceabilityDecision Traceability
Core questionWho recorded what result at what timeWhy it was analysed this way and why this judgment was made
Primary objectsReports, people, dates, batches, resultsData, analysis conditions, rules, judgments, verification
Primary roleCompliance proof and historical queryEngineering reproduction and decision reuse
Basis of judgmentOften scattered in email or personal experienceKept together with the analysis results
Subsequent verificationUsually ends when the issue is closedContinues to link action effectiveness and later performance
Knowledge valueProves that something happenedExplains why a certain action was taken
Long-term valueRecord (record asset)Engineering Knowledge Asset

When companies can gradually move from the Report Traceability of who measured, when, and what the result was, towards the Decision Traceability of why it was analysed this way, why this judgment was made, and whether it was later verified, quality data truly begins to transform from record assets to decision assets.
